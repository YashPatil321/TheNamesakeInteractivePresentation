## STEP 1: FORMAT SELECTION & VISION

**Format:** ✅ **Interactive Digital Story**

**Our creative vision in one sentence:**
We built "The Namesake Line," an animated website where a steam train carries the audience through Gogol Ganguli's life from 1961 to 2000, stopping at 15 "stations" that combine the story, our analysis, and videos of us acting out the biggest scenes.

**Target audience (beyond our classmates):**
Teens who are children of immigrants or who feel caught between two cultures, anyone whose name gets mispronounced or who has been told to "just pick an easier name," and teachers looking for ways to talk about identity and assimilation.

**Our "hook" ideas:**
1. **The Overcoat opening.** The site starts on a closed wool overcoat, a nod to "The Overcoat," the Nikolai Gogol story at the center of the book. Visitors write their own name on a "HELLO my name is" sticker, then the coat swings open and the train pulls in.
2. **Rewind to the crash.** At Gogol's birth, a "Why 'Gogol'?" button rewinds the train (with a VHS rewind effect) to 1961. The train crash plays out in black and white: the screen shakes, the cars tip off the track, and a flashlight finds the page from "The Overcoat."
3. **Live class question.** Before we start: "Raise your hand if you've ever gone by a different name at school."
4. **A personal ending.** The finale asks each visitor by name: "Who gave you your name?"

## STEP 2: CORE ANALYSIS FRAMEWORK

### A. The System Exposed

**What is the main system of bias/oppression your book reveals?**
**Pressure on immigrant families to assimilate.** American culture pushes immigrants and their children to shrink or erase their names, language, and traditions to fit in. It rarely looks like open hostility. It shows up as institutions that don't make room for other cultures, casual stereotypes, and the shame kids like Gogol learn to feel about their own heritage.

**Key evidence from the text:**

- **Quote/Example 1:** *"Being a foreigner, Ashima is beginning to realize, is a sort of lifelong pregnancy—a perpetual wait, a constant burden, a continuous feeling of out of sorts."* (Ch. 3, p. __). This shows the isolation and pressure immigrant parents live with.
- **Quote/Example 2:** The hospital won't discharge baby Gogol without a name on the birth certificate, even though by Bengali custom his great-grandmother is supposed to choose his "good name" and her letter is still in the mail. That American rule is the only reason "Gogol" becomes his official name (Ch. 2, p. __).
- **Quote/Example 3:** Before college, Gogol legally changes his name to "Nikhil." Afterward he feels like he is pretending, living as two people. The name change is the book's biggest symbol of assimilation (Ch. 5, p. __).

### B. The 4 I's of Oppression Analysis

**IDEOLOGICAL (beliefs/stereotypes that justify oppression):**
- *How it appears in the book:* The belief that "American" means white and that anyone Indian is a permanent outsider. At the Ratliffs' lake house in New Hampshire, a dinner guest assumes Gogol must never get sick in India because he's "Indian," and he has to point out that he grew up in Massachusetts (Ch. 6, p. __).
- *Real-world connection:* Asian Americans regularly get asked "Where are you *really* from?", the "perpetual foreigner" stereotype.

**INSTITUTIONAL (laws, policies, organizational practices):**
- *How it appears in the book:* The hospital's birth-certificate rule forces a name on Gogol. At kindergarten, the principal overrules his parents' plan to call him "Nikhil," so the school ends up deciding his name. Later he needs a court process to change his legal name.
- *Real-world connection:* Hiring studies find that résumés with non-white-sounding names get fewer callbacks, so many immigrants use anglicized names on job applications.

**INTERPERSONAL (individual actions between people):**
- *How it appears in the book:* Kids tease Gogol about his name. The family's mailbox is vandalized in a way that mocks their name. An English teacher's lesson on Nikolai Gogol's strange life humiliates Gogol in front of the class.
- *Real-world connection:* Teachers and classmates mispronounce or "nickname" students' names. Researchers Rita Kohli and Daniel Solórzano (2012, "Teachers, please learn our names!") found this affects how students see themselves. In 1987, a group calling itself the "Dotbusters" targeted Indian Americans in Jersey City.

**INTERNALIZED (when oppressed groups believe negative messages about themselves):**
- *How it appears in the book:* Gogol is embarrassed by his name, his parents' accents, and Bengali traditions. He changes his name, hides parts of himself around Maxine, and feels more at home with her family than his own. Moushumi pushes away her Bengali background in a similar way.
- *Real-world connection:* Children of immigrants often describe feeling "too American for home, too foreign for America," and some refuse to speak their parents' language.

### C. Breaking Point Analysis

**When does trauma/tension peak for your protagonist?**

**Specific scene/moment:** **Ashoke's death** (Ch. 7). Gogol gets the call that his father died suddenly of a heart attack in Cleveland. He flies there alone, identifies his father, and clears out the apartment.

**Why this is the climax:** Everything Gogol pushed away (his father, his name, his family's traditions) suddenly can't be taken back. He never read the book his father gave him, and the only person who knew the full story behind "Gogol" is gone. The conflict between his two identities peaks here.

**Emotional impact on character:** Guilt and grief. Gogol shaves his head as part of Bengali mourning, the first tradition he chooses for himself. He moves back toward his mother and sister and pulls away from Maxine, whose world suddenly feels like it has no room for his grief. It turns him back toward "Gogol."

### D. Resistance & Revolution

**How do characters fight back and/or find healing?**

**Individual actions:**
- At the end, Gogol finally opens *The Short Stories of Nikolai Gogol*, reads his father's inscription, and starts reading. He stops choosing between his two names.
- Ashima decides to split her year between India and America, building a life that belongs to both places instead of picking one.

**Community responses:**
The Bengali immigrant community acts as a stand-in family. Its members hold rice ceremonies, pujos, and holiday parties together, and they gather around Ashima after Ashoke dies. They keep their culture alive for each other in a new country.

**Systemic changes (if any):**
Almost none in the book. The institutions stay the same and the characters adapt, so the healing in this story is personal, not systemic. In real life, schools and workplaces increasingly offer name-pronunciation tools and "say my name right" campaigns.

### E. Mirror to Society

**What does this story reveal about our current world?**

**Connection to historical event (include researched source):**
The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler Act)** ended the quotas that had nearly shut out Asian immigrants. That opened the door for educated professionals like Ashoke, who settles in the U.S. just a few years after the law passed.

**Connection to current event (include researched source):**
Research keeps finding name-based discrimination in hiring. Large studies send out identical résumés with different names and find fewer callbacks for non-white names, so some immigrants still anglicize their names to get interviews.
*Source:* _______ (search "name discrimination hiring study résumé callbacks" and use a recent NBER study or major news article)

**What it shows about human nature:**
People want to belong so badly they'll hide parts of themselves to fit in. The identity we inherit is complicated, and we often understand our parents only after it's too late to tell them.

**Warning or hope for the future:**
**Both.** The warning: when society pressures people to erase where they come from, families lose connection across generations. The hope: Gogol shows it's never too late to accept your whole self. You don't have to choose between two cultures to belong.

## STEP 3: CREATIVE ELEMENTS PLANNING (Pick 1)

**Chosen: ✅ Interactive Component**

**What will the audience participate in:**
- **Ride the timeline.** Visitors move a train along a track, using arrow keys, a clicker, a swipe, or a click on any station. There are 15 stations from 1961 to 2000.
- **Station tickets.** Every stop opens a ticket-style card with a **Story** tab (what happens) and a **4 I's** tab (our oppression analysis with a real-world connection). Seven key stations also have a **Scene** tab with our acted video.
- **Gogol ⇄ Nikhil switch.** It stays locked until 1986. At that station a "PETITION GRANTED" stamp slams onto the screen and the switch unlocks. Flipping it changes the "inner voice" line on each card between Gogol's view and Nikhil's view.
- **The track shows his identity.** The rails glow orange while he's Gogol and blue while he's Nikhil. At the ending, two rails braid together to show both.
- **Rewind to 1961.** A button at his birth rewinds the train to the train crash, just as the book reveals the crash as a flashback.
- **The lost letter.** An envelope, standing in for the letter with his "good name" that never arrived, drifts across the sky. Clicking it shows messages about the name nobody ever learns.
- **Class votes.** At three stations the class votes by raising hands ("Would you use a new school name?", "Would you legally change your name to fit in?", "Does it make sense that Gogol feels more at home with Maxine's family?"). Then we reveal what Gogol actually did.
- **4 I's mode.** One button color-codes the whole timeline by type of oppression.
- **Unlockable ending.** The finale opens only after every station has been visited. The book opens, the inscription *"The man who gave you his name, from the man who gave you your name"* writes itself out, and the site asks the visitor, by the name they entered at the start, "Who gave you your name?"

**Soundtrack (bonus):**
1. "Yeh Jo Des Hai Tera" by A.R. Rahman (*Swades*): Ashima's homesickness for India
2. "Who Are You" by The Who: Gogol becoming "Nikhil"
3. "Father and Son" by Cat Stevens: the drive home when Ashoke tells the truth
4. "Fix You" by Coldplay: Ashoke's death and the family's grief
5. "Home" by Phillip Phillips: Gogol accepting who he is at the end

The website also has built-in sound effects: a train whistle, wheel clacks, the crash, and the stamp.

**Visual Aesthetic (bonus):**
- **Colors:** deep night-sky indigo as the background, **saffron orange for Gogol** and **sky blue for Nikhil** (they also color the track), purple for the ending when he is both, maroon Indian-railway train cars, and cream "ticket paper" for the cards. The 1961 and 1967 flashbacks switch to black and white.
- **Fonts:** Rozha One (a display font from an Indian type foundry) for years and titles, Spectral for the story text, and Courier Prime (typewriter style) for tickets, stamps, and labels.
- **Logo idea:** a rubber ticket stamp with a station code and year (like "CAM 1968"), plus a "HELLO my name is" name tag.
- **Scenery that changes by place:** palm trees and temples in Calcutta, church steeples in Cambridge, houses in the suburbs, Gothic towers at Yale, skyscrapers in New York, pines at the lake, smokestacks in Cleveland. Rain falls at Ashoke's death and snow on the last Christmas Eve.

## STEP 5: TECHNICAL REQUIREMENTS

**Equipment/Software Needed:**

**Recording equipment (phone, computer mic, etc.):** Phones for filming (landscape mode) and a phone or laptop mic for any narration. A flashlight for the train-crash lighting and to play train sound effects.
