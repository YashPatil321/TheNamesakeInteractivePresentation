## STEP 1: FORMAT SELECTION & VISION

**Format:** ✅ **Interactive Digital Story**

**Creative vision (one sentence):**
We're building an interactive website shaped like a train track that follows Gogol Ganguli's life from 1968 to 2000, mixing short videos of us acting out major scenes with clickable features that show how his name, and his fight over who he is, changes over time.

**Target audience (beyond classmates):**
Teens who are children of immigrants or who feel caught between two cultures, anyone whose name gets mispronounced or who has been told to "just pick an easier name," and teachers looking for ways to talk about identity and assimilation.

**Hook ideas:**
1. **"What's your name's story?"** The site opens by asking visitors to type their name and whether it has ever been mispronounced, shortened, or changed. Then it says: *"Gogol's name was an accident. Here's how it shaped his whole life."*
2. **The Overcoat.** The start screen is a closed coat that you click to open, based on "The Overcoat," the Nikolai Gogol story at the center of the book.
3. **Start with the crash.** We open on our black-and-white 1961 train crash video before anyone knows who Gogol is.
4. **Live poll.** "Raise your hand if you've ever gone by a different name at school." We ask the class right before we hit play.

## STEP 2: CORE ANALYSIS FRAMEWORK

### A. The System Exposed

**Main system of bias/oppression:**
**Pressure on immigrant families to assimilate.** American culture pushes immigrants and their children to shrink or erase their names, language, and traditions to fit in. It rarely looks like open hostility. It shows up as institutions that don't make room for other cultures, casual stereotypes, and the shame kids like Gogol learn to feel about their own heritage.

**Key evidence from the text:**

- **Quote/Example 1:** *"Being a foreigner, Ashima is beginning to realize, is a sort of lifelong pregnancy—a perpetual wait, a constant burden, a continuous feeling of out of sorts."* (Ch. 3, add page). This shows the isolation and pressure immigrant parents live with.
- **Quote/Example 2:** The hospital won't discharge baby Gogol without a name on the birth certificate, even though Bengali custom is to wait for a letter from his great-grandmother. That American rule is the only reason "Gogol" ever becomes his official name (Ch. 2, add page).
- **Quote/Example 3:** Gogol legally changes his name to "Nikhil" before college and afterward feels like he is pretending, living as two people. The name change is the book's biggest symbol of assimilation (Ch. 5, add page).

### B. The 4 I's of Oppression Analysis

**IDEOLOGICAL (beliefs/stereotypes):**
- *In the book:* The belief that "American" means white and that anyone Indian is a permanent outsider. At the lake house in New Hampshire, a dinner guest (Pamela) assumes Gogol must never get sick in India because he's "Indian," and Gogol has to point out that he grew up in Massachusetts (Ch. 6, check page).
- *Real-world connection:* Americans of Asian descent regularly get asked "Where are you *really* from?", the "perpetual foreigner" stereotype.

**INSTITUTIONAL (laws, policies, practices):**
- *In the book:* The hospital's birth-certificate rule forces a name on Gogol. At kindergarten the school registers him under the name the principal chooses, not his parents' choice. He later needs a court process to change his legal name.
- *Real-world connection:* Hiring studies find résumés with "ethnic-sounding" names get fewer callbacks, so many immigrants use anglicized names on job applications.

**INTERPERSONAL (individual actions):**
- *In the book:* Kids tease Gogol about his name. The family's mailbox name "GANGULI" is vandalized. The teacher's lesson on Nikolai Gogol's strange life humiliates Gogol in front of his class. Maxine's family treats his culture as something exotic.
- *Real-world connection:* Teachers and classmates mispronouncing or "nicknaming" students' names. During the 2020 election, a U.S. senator publicly mocked Kamala Harris's name at a rally.

**INTERNALIZED (believing negative messages about yourself):**
- *In the book:* Gogol is embarrassed by his name, his parents' accents, and Bengali traditions. He changes his name and hides parts of himself with Maxine, feeling more at home with her family than his own. Moushumi rejects her Bengali background in a similar way.
- *Real-world connection:* Children of immigrants often describe feeling "too American for home, too foreign for America," and some refuse to speak their parents' language.

### C. Breaking Point Analysis

**Specific scene/moment:** **Ashoke's death** (Ch. 7). Gogol gets the call that his father died suddenly of a heart attack in Cleveland. He flies there alone, identifies the body, and clears out his father's apartment.

**Why this is the climax:** Everything Gogol pushed away (his father, his name, his family's traditions) suddenly can't be taken back. He never read the book his father gave him, and the only person who knew the full story behind "Gogol" is gone. The conflict between his two identities peaks here.

**Emotional impact on character:** Guilt and grief. Gogol shaves his head as part of Bengali mourning, the first tradition he chooses for himself. He moves back toward his mother and sister and pulls away from Maxine, whose world suddenly feels shallow to him. It turns him back toward "Gogol."

### D. Resistance & Revolution

**Individual actions:**
- Gogol finally picks up *The Short Stories of Nikolai Gogol* at the end and begins reading, accepting both of his names and his father's story.
- Ashima decides to live six months in India and six in America, choosing a life that belongs to both places instead of picking one.

**Community responses:**
The Bengali immigrant community acts as a stand-in family. Its members hold rice ceremonies, pujos, and holiday parties together, and they gather around Ashima after Ashoke dies. They keep their culture alive for each other in a new country.

**Systemic changes (if any):**
Almost none in the book. The institutions stay the same and the characters adapt. (Point to discuss: healing in this story is personal, not systemic.) In real life, schools and workplaces increasingly offer name-pronunciation tools and "say my name right" campaigns.

### E. Mirror to Society

**Connection to historical event:**
The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler Act)** ended quotas that had nearly shut out Asian immigrants. That opened the door for educated professionals like Ashoke, who arrives in the U.S. around 1967, right after the law passed.
*Source to find:* the History.com article on the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, or the Migration Policy Institute's article on Indian immigrants in the U.S.

**Connection to current event:**
Research keeps finding name-based discrimination in hiring. Large audit studies that send identical résumés with different names show lower callback rates for non-white names, and some immigrants still anglicize their names to get interviews.
*Source to find:* search "name discrimination hiring study résumé callbacks" (look for NBER or major news coverage from the last few years).

**What it shows about human nature:**
People want to belong so badly they'll hide parts of themselves to fit in. The identity we inherit is complicated, and we usually understand our parents only after it's too late to tell them.

**Warning or hope for the future:**
**Both.** The warning: when society pressures people to erase where they come from, families lose connection across generations. The hope: Gogol shows it's never too late to accept your whole self. You don't have to choose between two cultures.

## STEP 3: CREATIVE ELEMENTS PLANNING (Pick 1)

**Chosen: ✅ Interactive Component** (it fits our website; the soundtrack and aesthetic below are optional extras)

**Interactive Component: what the audience participates in:**
- **Ride the timeline:** Visitors click or scroll a train along a track, stopping at "stations" for each major event (1961 crash → 2000 finale).
- **Gogol ⇄ Nikhil switch:** A toggle that unlocks at 1986. Flipping it changes how the story is told, showing his split identity.
- **Watch our scenes:** Short acted videos appear at key stations (crash, naming, kindergarten, the drive home, Ashoke's death, the ending).
- **"What would you do?" polls:** For example, "Would you go by Nikhil or Gogol at school?" Visitors vote before seeing what happened.
- **Unlockable ending:** The final station, the book inscription *"The man who gave you his name, from the man who gave you your name,"* opens only after you visit every station.
- **"Your name's story":** Visitors type their own name and its story at the start.

**Soundtrack (optional extra):**
1. "Yeh Jo Des Hai Tera" by A.R. Rahman (*Swades*): Ashima's homesickness for India
2. "Who Are You" by The Who: Gogol becoming "Nikhil" in college
3. "Father and Son" by Cat Stevens: the drive home when Ashoke tells the truth
4. "Fix You" by Coldplay: Ashoke's death and the family's grief
5. "Home" by Phillip Phillips: Gogol accepting who he is at the end

**Visual Aesthetic (optional extra):**
- **Colors:** warm saffron/orange for Gogol, India, and family scenes; cool blue for Nikhil, college, and New York; black-and-white for the 1961 flashback; cream "old paper" background.
- **Fonts:** a typewriter or book-style serif for quotes and titles (the book is central to the story) and a clean sans-serif for body text.
- **Logo idea:** the word "GOGOL" with "NIKHIL" peeking out from behind it, or a train ticket stamped with his name.
